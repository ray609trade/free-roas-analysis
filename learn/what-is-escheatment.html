Escheatment: how your money ends up with the state

3 min read · Updated 2026-07-25

What is escheatment and how does it affect retirement accounts?
Escheatment is the legal process by which an institution turns over an inactive account to the state after a dormancy period, typically three to five years of no contact. The state holds the money for you indefinitely in most cases — escheatment does not mean your money was forfeited or lost.

Dormancy comes first

An account becomes dormant when there is no owner-initiated activity and the institution cannot reach you. The clock length varies by state and property type, commonly three to five years. Returned mail is often what starts it, which is why an unchanged address after a move is the single most common cause of a lost account.

Then the state takes custody

The holder reports and remits the property to the state, which adds it to a public unclaimed property database. This is custodial, not confiscatory: in most states the money is held indefinitely until the rightful owner or heir claims it. There is generally no deadline.

Why this matters for retirement money

It explains why a 401(k) can be findable through a state treasurer rather than a retirement plan. Once a balance is cashed out of the plan and the payment goes stale, it stops being a retirement account and becomes ordinary unclaimed property — searchable by name, with no identity verification needed to look.

The real cost of waiting

Not forfeiture, but lost growth. Money sitting in a state fund or a dormant account is not compounding for your retirement. Anyone pressuring you to act immediately because your money is about to disappear is not being straight with you.

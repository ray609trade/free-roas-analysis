Glossary

The vocabulary of lost retirement accounts, in plain English. These terms come up constantly once you start searching, and knowing them makes every phone call easier.

You can claim your property yourself, for free. Your state or retirement plan does not charge you to file a claim for your own property, and every registry we reference is free to search. ReclaimWealth charges only for optional help doing the legwork.
Auto-rollover IRA (safe harbor IRA)An IRA opened in a former employee's name by their old plan to receive a forced-out balance. Legal and legitimate, but frequently unknown to the account owner because notice went to an old address.
Beneficiary designationThe form naming who inherits a retirement account. For retirement accounts this generally controls who receives the money, often overriding a will.
Defined benefit planA traditional pension promising a specified benefit in retirement, usually based on salary and years of service. Insured by the PBGC.
Defined contribution planA plan such as a 401(k) or 403(b) where contributions are defined and the eventual benefit depends on contributions and investment performance.
Dormancy periodThe length of time an account can go without owner-initiated contact before it is considered abandoned and reported to the state. Commonly three to five years, varying by state and property type.
EBSAThe Employee Benefits Security Administration, the Department of Labor division that oversees employer retirement plans. Its Benefits Advisors help former participants for free.
Escheated fundsMoney already transferred to a state's custody as unclaimed property. Searchable by name in public state databases, with no identity verification needed to look.
EscheatmentThe legal process by which an institution turns unclaimed property over to a state after a dormancy period. The state then holds it for the owner, in most cases indefinitely.
Force-out distributionA plan's removal of a former employee's small balance after they leave, either by cashing it out or moving it into an IRA, permitted without the participant's consent below certain balance thresholds.
Form 5500The annual report most employer retirement plans file with the federal government. Public and searchable, it identifies the sponsor and administrator, making it the best tool for tracing a plan after a merger or closure.
Orphan planA retirement plan left without an active sponsor, typically after the employer went out of business. The Department of Labor has procedures for terminating these and distributing benefits to participants.
PBGCThe Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation, a federal agency that insures traditional defined benefit pensions and holds unclaimed pension benefits from failed plans.
Plan administratorThe party legally responsible for operating the plan and answering participant requests. Named on the plan's Form 5500 filing — usually who you actually need to contact.
Plan sponsorThe employer or organization that establishes and maintains a retirement plan for its employees.
RecordkeeperThe firm that maintains participant accounts and provides statements and the login portal — Fidelity, Empower, Vanguard and similar. Recordkeepers change over time, which is a common reason a login stops working.
SECURE 2.0 ActFederal retirement legislation enacted in 2022 that, among many changes, directed the Department of Labor to create the Retirement Savings Lost & Found database.
TPA (third-party administrator)A firm hired to handle a plan's day-to-day administration and compliance. Common with small-business plans, and often the party holding the records you need.
Vested balanceThe portion of a retirement account you own outright and keep when you leave. Your own contributions are always fully vested; employer contributions may vest over a schedule.
